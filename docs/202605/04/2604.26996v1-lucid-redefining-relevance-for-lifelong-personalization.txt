## Abstract 

Current approaches to lifelong personalization operationalize relevance through semantic proximity, causing them to miss essential user informa-tion from topically unrelated interactions. To address this gap, we introduce LUCid, a benchmark designed to measure situational user-centric relevance in personalization. The benchmark consists of 1,936 realistic queries paired with interaction histories from up to 500 sessions. Across multiple architec-tures, our experiments show significant performance collapse when rele-vant context must be surfaced from semantically distant history: retrieval recall drops to near zero on the hardest instances, and response alignment remains near 50% even for state-of-the-art models such as Gemini-3-Flash, GPT-5.4, and Claude Haiku. These results expose a fundamental mismatch between the notion of relevance encoded by current systems and the sit-uational relevance required for personalization, with direct implications for robustness and safety when critical user attributes remain undetected. LUCid enables the systematic evaluation of whether current models can surface situationally-relevant user information from previous interactions, and serves as a step toward realigning personalization with user-centered relevance. 

## 1 Introduction 

People vary widely in their goals, preferences, and contexts, even within the same task or prompt (Sorensen et al., 2024). As they interact with LLM-powered conversational systems, they generate rich, evolving interaction histories that reflect these individual differences (Zhao et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Effective lifelong personalization therefore requires models to reason over this history; without it, conversational agents default to generic knowledge, producing responses that are not only unhelpful (Jandaghi et al., 2024) but potentially unsafe (Wu et al., 2026; Hao et al., 2026), undermining user trust over time. Given the cost and context-window limitations of full-history conditioning (Wu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025), RAG and its advanced variants, including GraphRAG (Peng et al., 2024) and memory-augmented approaches (Tan et al., 2025b; Tang et al., 2026), have emerged as the dominant paradigm for scalable personalization. Alongside these methods, several benchmarks have been developed to evaluate whether systems can surface and utilize relevant user history (Zhao et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025a;b). However, both methods and benchmarks share a critical underlying assumption that relevance is synony-mous with semantic proximity. Retrieval methods encode this assumption via semantic similarity scoring functions, while benchmarks reinforce it by constructing evaluation in-stances where relevant context and the current query share sufficient semantic overlap–—a bias we quantify through a new metric, Proximity Advantage (PA). This assumption is critically flawed for true lifelong personalization. Meaningful user signals frequently appear in historical sessions that are topically distant from the present query (Figure 1). Information retrieval research captures this through situational relevance ,which defines relevance by usefulness to the user’s task rather than semantic overlap (Borlund, 2003). Using LUCid, we ask: What notion of relevance do current lifelong personalization systems encode, and can they reliably identify and utilize latent user context? Our experiments evaluate state-of-the-art approaches spanning long-context conditioning, RAG-based memory architectures, and reranking systems. Our results demonstrate that existing systems largely encode relevance as semantic proximity, suffering significant performance collapse when user context must be inferred from semantically distant history, with retrieval recall dropping to near zero on hard instances and response alignment failing to exceed 50% even for state-of-the-art models such as GPT, Qwen and Claude. ## 2 Related Work 

Methods for Lifelong Personalization. Current approaches fall into three paradigms. Long-context methods directly condition on full interaction histories (Zhao et al., 2025; An et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024), but face computational bottlenecks and needle-in-a-haystack failures (Hsieh et al., 2024). RAG-based methods (Lewis et al., 2020) address this by retriev-ing the top-k semantically similar sessions, with extensions such as GraphRAG (Han et al., 2025; Edge et al., 2025) enabling multi-hop reasoning over structured entity-relation graphs. Memory-augmented systems (Tan et al., 2025b; Packer et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025; Zhou & Han, 2025; Tang et al., 2026) further compress histories into queryable memory banks. Critically, all three paradigms ultimately rely on semantic similarity at the retrieval frontier—whether over raw sessions, graph entry points/traversals, or compressed memory units—preserving the same proximity bottleneck we identify in Section 3. 

Benchmarks for Lifelong Personalization. Existing benchmarks have progressively scaled in scope. Early work such as LaMP (Salemi et al., 2024) and LongLaMP (Kumar et al., 2024) cover personalized NLP tasks, and LoCoMo (Maharana et al., 2024) targets long-term dialogue memory. More recent benchmarks probe dynamic user modeling across longer histories, including LongMemEval (Wu et al., 2025), PrefEval (Zhao et al., 2025), PersonaMem (Jiang et al., 2025b;a), and PersonaBench (Tan et al., 2025a). Closest to our setting, ImplexConv (Li et al., 2025) introduces semantically diverse multi-session signals requiring implicit reasoning, but frames queries from a third-person perspective, stopping short of first-person personalization. Across all benchmarks, relevant context remains largely recoverable via semantic overlap with the query, leaving the harder regime of latent, situationally relevant but semantically distant user context entirely unaddressed: the gap LUCid is designed to fill (see Appendix 6 for a detailed benchmark comparison table). 

## 3 Preliminaries 

3.1 Lifelong Personalization: Problem Setting 

Let H = {s1, s2, . . . , sn} be a lifelong interaction history consisting of n sessions, and let qn+1

be a new user query. A personalization system aims to generate a response y by conditioning on a subset of the history: P(y | qn+1, ϕ(H)) , where ϕ is a selection or compression function. 

3.2 The Contrastive Bottleneck 

The most common implementation of ϕ is the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework (Lewis et al., 2020), which selects a subset of k sessions from H via a scoring function. Dense retrievers operationalize this by encoding queries and sessions into a shared embedding space, where relevance is approximated by vector similarity. While such retrievers could in principle encode true relevance , their notion of relevance is fundamentally shaped by their training objective and data. Models like Contriever (Izacard et al., 2022) are optimized using a contrastive loss (e.g., InfoNCE) to minimize the distance between a query 

q and a positive context z+ (the relevant document):

Since spans are drawn from the same document, they are semantically related, causing their embeddings to cluster in vector space. Computing cosine similarity between these representations therefore operationalizes relevance as semantic proximity. ## 4 LUCid 

LUCid is designed to break the “proximity bias” identified in Section 3.3, enabling systems to reason about situational relevance that goes beyond semantic proximity. Following Section 3.1, each evaluation instance in LUCid is defined as (H, qn+1, A∗), where A∗ denotes the latent user attribute that must be inferred from the interaction history to produce the correct personalized response y∗ = f (qn+1, A∗). This attribute is not explicitly stated in the query nor in any individual session and must therefore be inferred. Consequently, we define the 

Latent User Context CL as the set of sessions in H from which A∗ can be inferred. As we will see, by design CL often have low semantic similarity to the user query. Thus, solving a LUCid instance requires identifying and reasoning over sessions that contain user contexts that are situationally necessary for the response despite their weak semantic similarity to the query. To accommodate varying computational constraints and research objectives, we introduce four benchmark variants: LUCid-C (controlled reranking probe, 30 sessions, ∼47K tokens), 

LUCid-S (Small, 50 sessions, ∼64K tokens), LUCid-B (Base, 200 sessions, ∼270K tokens), and LUCid-L (Large, 500 sessions, ∼620K tokens), with the latter providing a high-difficulty setting to push the boundaries of long-context reasoning. Across all variants, the benchmark consists of 1,936 evaluation queries, each paired with histories manually designed to reflect the dynamics of real-world conversational logs. We further define a challenging subset, 

LUCid-HARD , consisting of 856 instances from LUCid-B where the relevant sessions lie in the extreme semantic tail of the interaction history. We describe the dataset construction in the next section. 

4.1 Constructing LUCid 

We construct LUCid through a multi-stage pipeline that mirrors real-world conversational dynamics. Our pipeline consists of four stages: (1) query synthesis, (2) relevant session construction, (3) irrelevant session curation, and (4) interaction history aggregation. 

Query Synthesis. We first identify a set of user-specific dimensions along which a correct personalized response varies meaningfully (Table 2). Guided by these dimensions, we synthesize queries using three complementary strategies: (1) extraction and adaptation of naturally occurring user queries from real-world conversational logs such as UltraChat and ShareGPT (Ding et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2023) (N=125); (2) adoption or light modification 4Preprint. Under review. of queries from existing datasets aligned to specific dimensions, including SituatedQA (Zhang & Choi, 2021) for geographic and location-aware queries (N=273), PersonaMem (Jiang et al., 2025b) for health-related queries (N=561), PrefEval (Zhao et al., 2025) for movie recommendation queries (N=75), and SafeBench (Ying et al., 2024) and CASE-Bench (Sun et al., 2025) for safety-critical age-sensitive queries (N=43); and (3) manual writing of queries for dimensions not covered by existing sources (N=859). All queries are manually inspected to ensure (i) clarity, (ii) realism, and (iii) presence of personalization cues that signal a user’s expectation of a tailored response. Relevant Session Construction. Relevant sessions are those comprising the latent user context CL for a given query (Section 4). Drawing on conversational logs from UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023) and ShareGPT (Ouyang et al., 2023) to inform structure and linguistic style, sessions are sourced via three methods: (1) direct extraction from logs where a session naturally surfaces a target attribute; (2) dimension-value translation, where an extracted session is adapted to reflect a different value within the same dimension (e.g., adapting a session revealing US residency to indicate UK residency); and (3) LLM-based synthesis via a self-chat pipeline (Xu et al., 2023) for attribute-value pairs underrepresented in existing logs. For each dimension-value pair (e.g., age-group: teen), we construct a group of sessions spanning diverse topics, each revealing the same latent attribute through indirect signals: a user’s age, for instance, may surface across sessions about a first concert experience or a generational event. Some instances require multi-session reasoning, where no single session suffices: a user who consistently resists formal responses signals a preference for casual communication; one whose sessions span compilers, data mining, and tokenization plausi-bly indicates a computer science affiliation. This mirrors real-world personalization, where user identity emerges gradually across a history rather than through isolated disclosures. 

Irrelevant Session Construction. Irrelevant sessions serve as realistic distractors: they contain no personally attributable user information and neither contribute latent context nor interact with or contradict signals from relevant sessions. We manually inspect over 10,000 sessions from UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023) and ShareGPT (Ouyang et al., 2023) and retain approximately 700 that satisfy this criterion, predominantly general knowledge-seeking or curiosity-driven conversations spanning diverse topics (e.g., wildlife behavior, general science, social phenomena). 

Interaction History Aggregation. For each evaluation instance — defined by a query, its personalization dimension, and a target attribute value — we select the corresponding relevant session group and compute the similarity between each session and the query. We retain the most semantically dissimilar session(s), specifically the single most dissimilar for single-signal instances and the five most dissimilar for multi-session instances. From the irrelevant pool, we select the K sessions most topically similar to the query, where K is determined by the benchmark variant (C, S, B, or L). The two sets are combined to form the final haystack for that evaluation instance, as illustrated in Figure 2 for both single-session (left) and multi-session (right) instances. This construction is central to LUCid’s diagnostic intent: relevant sessions are selected for low similarity while irrelevant sessions are selected for high similarity, explicitly decoupling semantic similarity from true relevance. Crucially, because the two pools are indepen-dent, the relevant session does not necessarily occupy the lowest-similarity position in the 5Preprint. ## 5 Experiments and Results 

Using LUCid, we design experiments to systematically probe the notion of relevance encoded by current personalization architectures—specifically, whether they can identify and utilize latent user context . We focus on three core research questions (RQs): RQ1: To what extent can current retrieval systems identify latent user contexts? RQ2: What notion of relevance do rerankers/LLMs encode? RQ3: Can long-context LLMs natively surface and condition on latent user attributes?

## 6 Lessons Learned 

6.1 Situational Relevance Should Be the Target for Lifelong Personalization 

Relevance has been a central and contested concept in IR for decades (Saracevic, 1975; Borlund, 2003). Borlund (2003) distinguishes between algorithmic, topical, pertinence-based, and situational relevance (SR), with SR widely regarded as the most realistic form: it evaluates information with respect to the user’s actual task and context rather than lexical or topical overlap alone. Since objects may be situationally relevant without being algorithmically relevant, and vice versa (Borlund, 2003), SR is the appropriate target for lifelong personalization. The purpose of personalization is to make models more helpful for particular users, under their goals, constraints, and contexts. Once framed this way, relevance should be defined not by whether a past interaction is topically adjacent to the current request, but by whether it is useful for generating the right response for that user. However, current personalization pipelines encode a narrower notion: while generation is evaluated using user-centered metrics, retrieval remains governed by semantic similarity, creating a structural mismatch where systems are optimized for personalized outputs but rely on mechanisms that systematically exclude the user context required to produce them. We argue for placing situational relevance at the forefront of personalization—at retrieval time, by prioritizing context that is situationally necessary even when semantically distant; at generation time, by evaluating responses on their ability to utilize that context appropriately and safely. Under this framing, LUCid is a step toward realigning personalization with the user-centered notion of relevance long established in IR. 8Preprint. Under review. 

6.2 Under Ambiguity, LLMs Default to WEIRD Assumptions 

In the absence of user-specific signals, models resolve ambiguity by defaulting to dominant pretraining priors — producing U.S.-centric responses to location-sensitive queries rather than acknowledging uncertainty or eliciting missing context (see Appendix D.4 for the full breakdown). While such defaults may be corrected through interaction, they are problematic when users implicitly assume the model understands their context, leading to misaligned or exclusionary responses. We posit that in such cases of unresolved ambiguity, a preferable strategy is to adopt an Overton pluralistic approach (Sorensen et al., 2024), where models present a spectrum of plausible interpretations or elicit missing context rather than committing to a single implicit assumption. 

6.3 Benchmark-Driven Alignment Does Not Generalize to Situational Safety 

Under Gold conditioning, models achieve near-perfect performance on age-sensitive queries related to alcohol and explicit content (e.g., 100% for Qwen, see Appendix D.3 for the full breakdown), yet systematically fail on entertainment recommendation queries targeting the same age group (e.g., 22.67% for Qwen), often recommending R- and MA-rated content to minors (see Figure 6.3). This disparity reflects a structural bias in current alignment pipelines: models are highly optimized for harm categories well-represented in safety benchmarks, but exhibit inconsistent behavior where appropriateness is less explicitly defined. Since our explicit-content queries are derived directly from SafeBench and CASE-Bench (Sun et al., 2025; Ying et al., 2024), models appear to have internalized benchmark-specific surface patterns rather than a generalizable principle of age-appropriateness. Addressing this requires moving beyond benchmark-driven alignment toward frameworks that treat safety as situational and user-specific — not reducible to a fixed set of harm categories.

## 7 Conclusion 

We introduced LUCid, a benchmark that evaluates situational relevance in lifelong person-alization by explicitly decoupling relevant user context from semantic proximity. Our exper-iments reveal a two-stage bottleneck: at retrieval, all systems—spanning RAG, memory-augmented architectures, and rerankers—collapse to near-zero recall on hard instances; at generation, state-of-the-art models (Gemini, GPT, Claude, Qwen) still struggle to condition on latent attributes even when the relevant session is directly provided. Beyond these core failures, models default to WEIRD-aligned priors under ambiguity and exhibit benchmark-driven inconsistencies in safety-critical settings. These findings expose a fundamental mismatch between the relevance current systems encode and what lifelong personalization demands, motivating a shift toward situational relevance at both retrieval and generation. We offer LUCid as a step toward that realignment. 

## 8 Limitations 

LUCid currently covers six user dimensions and multi-session reasoning across up to five sessions; extending to additional attributes such as disability or socioeconomic context, inter-dimension interactions, and longer evidence chains are natural directions for future work. Our evaluation covers representative architectures across dominant paradigms—a broader empirical comparison was precluded by cost, though we provide a theoretical account of why the proximity bottleneck extends to broader retrieval classes in Appendix B.4. 9Preprint. ## Ethics Statement 

LUCid is constructed from publicly available conversational logs (UltraChat, ShareGPT) and existing benchmarks. No personally identifiable information was collected or retained. All user attributes (age group, religion, health constraints, geographic location, domain affiliation, and communication style) are synthetic and assigned to fictional personas; they do not correspond to or derive from real individuals. Our benchmark deliberately incorporates sensitive user dimensions, including age, religion, and health, as these are precisely the attributes that matter most for safe and personalized generation. We emphasize that these dimensions are included to expose failures in current systems, not to enable or encourage their exploitation. Researchers using LUCid should be mindful of these sensitivities when reporting results or building systems evaluated on this benchmark. Our findings reveal that current models exhibit benchmark-driven alignment inconsistencies in safety-critical settings, most notably recommending age-inappropriate content to minors even when the user’s age is explicitly provided. We surface these failures to motivate more robust and generalizable safety alignment, and encourage the community to treat safety as situational and user-specific rather than reducible to a fixed set of harm categories. LUCid is intended for research evaluation of lifelong personalization systems and is not de-signed for adversarial probing of deployed systems or for identifying exploitable safety gaps in production models. We release the benchmark and curation pipeline to facilitate repro-ducible research and encourage extensions to additional user dimensions and demographic groups currently underrepresented in personalization evaluation. We hope LUCid encourages the development of personalization systems that are more equitable, context-aware, and genuinely user-centered.

## A Extended Related Work 

A.1 Current Methods for Lifelong Personalization 

Current methods for lifelong personalization can be broadly categorized into three paradigms: long context methods, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and augmented memory methods. Long context methods directly input extensive user histories into models’ context windows (Zhao et al., 2025; An et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024); however, scaling user history introduces significant computational bottlenecks and suffer the well-documented “needle-in-a-haystack” failures (Hsieh et al., 2024). To mitigate context limitations, RAG (Lewis et al., 2020) methods conditioning the model’s responses on only the top-k most semantically relevant user sessions. Extensions such as GraphRAG (Han et al., 2025; Edge et al., 2025; Han et al., 2026; Xiang et al., 2026) organize interaction histories into structured entity–relation graphs to enable multi-hop reasoning across sessions. Memory-augmented systems (Tan et al., 2025b; Packer et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025; Zhou & Han, 2025; Tang et al., 2026) further consolidate histories into structured, queryable memory units via memory agents and hierarchical compression. Augmented memory methods treat personalization and memory as an evolving state, using systems However, both RAG and GraphRAG approximate relevance as semantic proximity at the retrieval frontier: flat RAG scores sessions by embedding-space similarity to the query; GraphRAG encodes proximity bias into both entry-point selection and in some implementations graph traversal (Edge et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2024), causing it to compound at each hop; and memory-augmented systems surface their compressed units using same backbone (flat RAG, GraphRAG, or a dual-stage retrieve-then-rerank pipeline) thus, preserving the bottleneck on a compressed index rather than eliminating it. We evaluate LUCid across a representative set of these approaches as well as provide a theoretical account of their structural limitations in Section 3. 

A.2 Benchmarks for Evaluating Lifelong Personalization 

Benchmarks for lifelong personalization have progressively expanded in scope and difficulty. Early work such as LaMP (Salemi et al., 2024) introduced personalized language modeling across diverse NLP tasks, while LongLaMP (Kumar et al., 2024) extended this to long-form generation. LoCoMo (Maharana et al., 2024) evaluates long-term memory over sustained multi-session dialogues, and PersoBench (Afzoon et al., 2026) assesses response-level per-sonalization quality using existing persona-aware datasets. More recent benchmarks target long-horizon and dynamic user modeling: LongMemEval (Wu et al., 2025) probes five core memory abilities across multi-session histories; PrefEval (Zhao et al., 2025) evaluates explicit and implicit preference following in contexts up to 100K tokens; PersonaMem (Jiang et al., 2025b;a) emphasizes tracking evolving user profiles across temporally grounded sessions; and PersonaBench (Tan et al., 2025a) tests understanding of personal information derived from synthetic private user documents. Closest to our setting in terms of relaxing semantic proximity, ImplexConv (Li et al., 2025) introduces multi-session histories with semantically diverse signals requiring implicit reasoning; however, it frames queries from a third-person perspective and does not directly probe whether systems can surface situationally relevant user context for first-person personalization. Across all other benchmarks, relevant informa-tion remains largely recoverable via semantic overlap with the query, leaving unaddressed the harder regime in which functionally necessary user context is latent, semantically dis-tant, and only situationally relevant. We summarize a detailed comparison of existing benchmarks and LUCid across multiple dimensions in Table 6, including history length, number of queries, reasoning type, and proximity advantage.

B.2 Retrieval-Augmented Generation 

The most common implementation of ϕ is the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework (Lewis et al., 2020), which selects a subset of k sessions from H via a scoring function: ϕ(H, qn+1) = top-ksi ∈H (score (qn+1, si)) 

Early retrievers such as DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) learn these scores from supervised question-answer pairs, while more recent systems such as Contriever (Izacard et al., 2022) use large-scale contrastive objectives for more general-purpose embeddings. In both cases, relevance reduces to vector similarity in the embedding space: score (qn+1, si) = E(qn+1)⊤ E(si) (2) As we show next, this shared reliance on embedding-space proximity has fundamental consequences for lifelong personalization. 

B.3 The Contrastive Bottleneck 

While dense retrievers could, in principle, encode true relevance , their utility is shaped by their training objective. Models like Contriever are optimized using a contrastive loss (e.g., InfoNCE) to minimize the distance between a query q and a positive context z+:

L = − log exp (sim (q, z+)/τ)

∑j∈{ z+,z1,..., zm } exp (sim (q, zj)/τ) (3) The bottleneck arises from the training data sampling strategy: z+ is constructed from another span of the same document via augmentations such as the Inverse Cloze Task or independent cropping. Since spans are drawn from the same document, they inevitably share topical content, causing their embeddings to cluster in vector space. Computing cosine similarity between these representations therefore operationalizes relevance as topical proximity. In a lifelong personalization setting, this implicitly forces the retriever to treat 

latent user contexts as a negative sample zj, preventing it from surfacing the signals necessary for safe and personalized generation. 

B.4 Beyond Standard RAG 

Several works augment standard RAG to address its granularity and structural limitations. Memory-augmented systems (Packer et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025b) compress interaction histories into structured, queryable memory units; however, these units are still surfaced at query time via embedding-space similarity search, preserving the proximity bottleneck on a compressed index rather than eliminating it. Graph-augmented systems (Han et al., 2025; Edge et al., 2025; Han et al., 2026; Xiang et al., 2026) organize history into entity-relation graphs to enable multi-hop reasoning across sessions. However, both entry-point node selection and, in some implementations, graph traversal are guided by similarity-based scoring function (Edge et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2024), causing proximity bias to compound at each hop. These augmentations thus share the same structural failure: without a richer notion of relevance at the retrieval frontier, latent user context remains systematically inaccessible. 16 Preprint. ## C Data Synthesis and Analysis 

C.1 Further Construction details 

Our dataset was constructed in a systematic four-stage process that mirrors real-world conversations between users and chatbots: (1) query synthesis, (2) relevant session synthesis, (3) irrelevant session synthesis, and (4) aggregation. 

C.1.1 1. Query synthesis 

We identify six user-specific dimensions (domain, age group, location, religion, medical condition, and communication style) and synthesize queries via three strategies: extraction from UltraChat and ShareGPT (Ding et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2023) (N=125), modification of existing dataset queries, and manual authoring (N=859). 

Domain. Domain queries are constructed around polysemous terms , words whose meaning or application varies across professional fields (e.g., “entropy” in physics vs. information theory). We use WordNet (Princeton University, 2010) to identify such terms via their sense distinctions across domains, then prompt an LLM to generate query variations around each term, followed by manual review for validity. The full pipeline is illustrated in Figure 3. 

Age Group. We identify three sub-dimensions where appropriate responses vary meaning-fully by age: health, fitness, and entertainment. Fitness and health queries are generated via LLM prompting; entertainment queries are extracted from PrefEval (Zhao et al., 2025). Safety-critical age-sensitive queries are sourced from SafeBench (Ying et al., 2024) and CASE-Bench (Sun et al., 2025) (N=43). All queries are manually reviewed and rewritten where necessary to ensure that a generic response would be insufficient — that is, a correct response requires knowing the user’s age group. 

Country/Location. Queries are extracted from SituatedQA (Zhang & Choi, 2021) and supplemented with LLM-generated queries verified through a human-in-the-loop process. All queries are rewritten to embed implicit location cues, ensuring that a correct personalized response requires inferring the user’s geographic context rather than defaulting to a generic answer. 

Religion and Communication Style. Queries are manually authored and augmented with LLM generation, covering topics where correct responses vary meaningfully based on the user’s religious norms or preferred communication style. 18 Preprint. Under review. 

Medical/Health Condition. Queries are extracted from PersonaMem (Jiang et al., 2025b) and supplemented with LLM-generated queries, covering health and lifestyle topics where advice varies based on the user’s medical condition. 

C.1.2 Relevant Session Synthesis 

Relevant sessions are constructed from UltraChat, and ShareGPT (Ding et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2023). We first apply LLM-based screening to identify sessions from which at least one target dimension can be implicitly inferred, followed by manual verification to confirm that the latent attribute is recoverable without explicit disclosure. To address representational imbalances across dimension values, we apply dimension-value translation: existing sessions are adapted to reflect underrepresented values by swapping entities or injecting implicit cues. For location, U.S.-centric sessions are localized to other regions (e.g., replacing U.S. store references with Nigerian equivalents). For age group, senior citizen sessions are augmented with temporal cues (e.g., retirement references, allusions to historical events) and teenager sessions with school-related markers and implicit birth year indicators. For religion, general sessions are adapted by inserting faith-based contextual cues (e.g., references to Ramadan). Medical/health condition sessions are sourced directly from PersonaMem (Jiang et al., 2025b). Domain and communication style sessions are synthesized via the self-chat pipeline described in Figure 7. For communication style, a user’s preference is distributed across multiple sessions, each reflecting the same stylistic tendency in a different conversational context, such that inferring the attribute requires multi-session reasoning rather than a single salient disclosure. Both domain and communication-style query instances in LUCid requires reasoning across multiple sessions to infer the user attribute A∗.

C.1.3 Irrelevant Session Curation 

Irrelevant sessions serve as realistic distractors within the interaction history, containing no personally attributable user information and neither contributing latent context nor contradicting signals from relevant sessions. We apply LLM-based screening to rapidly filter sessions from UltraChat and ShareGPT (Ding et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2023) where no user-specific information could be inferred, followed by manual inspection of over 10,000 candidate sessions to verify that none reveal information across any of our six target dimensions. This process yields a pool of 700 irrelevant sessions, predominantly general knowledge-seeking and day-to-day conversations spanning diverse topics. 

C.1.4 Interaction History Aggregation 

The aggregation process follows the pipeline described in Section 4.1 and illustrated in Figure 4.
